Personal build

A desk fan that finds your face and follows it.

Three builds, each one replacing the last: a thermostat, then ultrasonic tracking, then computer vision. The tracking math was the easy part. The real work was power, electrical noise and timing.

How it works

A control loop that fits in one line of serial

A webcam frame goes to MediaPipe's face detector. How far the face sits from the centre of the frame becomes an error in X and Y, and that error maps onto a pan angle and a tilt angle.

Those two numbers travel down a USB cable at 115200 baud as a single line: P95 T110 F1. Pan 95°, tilt 110°, face present. The Arduino keeps the most recent command and eases each servo toward it a couple of degrees per tick, so the head glides instead of snapping. A dead-band near the centre stops it hunting when you sit still.

The same line switches the fan through a logic-level MOSFET, on while a face is in view and for a second and a half after it leaves. If the serial link goes quiet for two seconds the Arduino shuts the fan off by itself, so a crashed script on the laptop can't leave it running.

Three generations

Each version solved the last one's real problem

01

Thermostat

A DHT11 sensor switching the fan on above 80 °F through a transistor, with a flyback diode across the motor. The first version chattered on and off at the threshold, so I added hysteresis: on at 80, off at 77.

Took away Switching an inductive load, and why a threshold needs two numbers rather than one.

02

Ultrasonic tracking

Two HC-SR04 sensors in a shallow V. Compare the left and right distances, steer toward whichever reads closer, and the fan keeps itself pointed at you. Raw, it jerked constantly, so the final version used a median-of-three filter, a 12 cm floor to throw out impossible readings, 60 ms between pings to stop the sensors hearing each other, and 10° steps inside a ±45° limit.

Took away Sensor data is never clean. Most of the code that matters is filtering.

03

Computer vision Current

Ultrasonic sensors can tell you something is there, not that it is a person, and they only work in one dimension. Swapping them for face detection gave real two-axis tracking of a head. The vision runs in Python on a laptop today and talks to the Arduino over serial; the next step moves it onto a Raspberry Pi with the camera riding on the fan head.

Took away Prototyping the vision on a laptop webcam first meant the algorithm was already working before any new hardware was bought.

What broke

Four failures worth keeping

None of these were in the tracking code. Every one came down to power, noise or timing, and each took a different kind of debugging to find.

The board keeps rebooting.
Symptom
Garbled serial output with the startup banner repeating every couple of seconds, and servos that twitched and died.
Cause
One 9 V battery feeding the Arduino, the servos and the motor. Servo inrush dragged the rail under the brownout threshold and reset the board.
Fix
Split the supplies. A 12 V adapter with a buck converter for the servos, and the Arduino on its own USB power, so motor current can't touch the logic rail.
The fan switches itself on.
Symptom
The fan pulsed every two seconds, in time with the resets.
Cause
The resets came too fast for the board to reach the fan code at all, which meant something else was driving the gate. Nothing held the MOSFET gate low during boot, so it floated high, the fan surged, and the rail collapsed again.
Fix
A 10 kΩ pull-down from gate to ground, with 220 Ω in series to protect the pin. The timing of the symptom was the clue that found it.
Tracking freezes after ten seconds.
Symptom
Smooth at first, then the servos updated once every ten seconds or so.
Cause
The firmware echoed a debug line back for every command it received, thirty times a second. The serial buffers filled, writes started blocking, and the blocked write stalled the vision loop.
Fix
Dropped the echo, throttled the sender to 25 Hz, and set a write timeout so a slow write drops one frame instead of freezing the loop.
The sensors are lying.
Symptom
One ultrasonic sensor returned 999 for no echo while the other reported an object 2 cm away that wasn't there.
Cause
The two sensors were hearing each other's pings, and one had a cold solder joint.
Fix
Median filtering, a minimum-distance floor, spacing the pings apart in time, and reflowing the joint. Swapping sensors between channels is what proved it was the joint and not the code.
Enclosure

Three printed parts, modelled parametrically

The housing is written in OpenSCAD rather than drawn, so every component position comes from a measured dimension at the top of the file. Change the size of the buck converter and its tray, and everything around it, update on the next render.

The model carries its own fit checks. Assertions compare each component's footprint against its neighbours and stop the render with a message naming the limit, so a part that would collide fails at the keyboard instead of after six hours of printing. All three parts print without support.

Base

180 × 140 × 45 mm. Vent slots, cable ports, trays and standoffs for every board.

Lid

A recessed pocket drops the pan servo flush into the top, so the head sits on the surface.

Fan cradle

Carries the fan, bolts to the tilt platform on slots, and mounts the camera above.

Wiring

One adapter in, three rails out

A single 12 V supply runs the fan directly and feeds a buck converter that makes the 6 V servo rail. The Arduino never carries motor current. It sends signals and shares a ground, which is the arrangement the brownouts taught me to build.

Next

Where it goes from here

Moving the vision onto a Raspberry Pi puts the camera on the fan head, which closes the loop: instead of mapping a face's position in a fixed webcam view onto an angle, the fan turns until the face is centred in its own view. The protoboard becomes a PCB, and the whole thing stops needing a laptop.
